Final Project Guidelines

MATH 4780 / MSSC 5780 — Regression Analysis

This is the latest version of project guideline. It may be revised later.

Overview

The goal of the final project is to demonstrate that you can think critically about regression models, use them correctly, and communicate what you learn. You may choose either of the following options:

  • Option A — Data Analysis: Investigate a meaningful question using regression analysis of a real data set.
  • Option B — New Regression Method: Learn a regression method not covered in class, explain why it is useful, and evaluate it through a computational study.

Neither option is inherently better or worth more points. You will be evaluated on the quality of your reasoning, analysis, and communication—not simply on model complexity.

One-Page Proposal

Include:

  1. Title and members: Project title, names, course, and whether you selected Option A or B.
  2. Motivation: What question or methodological problem is interesting, and why does it matter?
  3. Data or method:
  • Option A, identify the data source, response, and potential predictors.
  • Option B, identify the new method and the limitation of traditional regression it addresses.
  1. Analysis plan: Explain how you will fit models and evaluate or compare them (including a planned graph, diagnostic, or experiment).

Your plan may evolve or change as you learn more from the data or method.

The proposal should be concrete enough to show that the project is feasible.

Project Requirements

Option A — Data Analysis

Use regression to answer a specific, meaningful question with a real data set. Your project should:

  1. State the question and introduce the data. Describe where the data came from, what the observations represent, and the response and predictors. Discuss important data limitations.
  2. Explore the data visually. Include informative plots that guide modeling decisions, rather than charts added only for decoration.
  3. Build and justify your model(s). Explain important decisions about variables, transformations, interactions, and model selection. Begin with a reasonable regression model and compare an alternative when informative.
  4. Check model adequacy. Use relevant residual and assumption checks, leverage/influence diagnostics, and collinearity diagnostics. Explain what you found and whether changes are needed. Not every diagnostic is equally applicable to every model.
  5. Answer the question and evaluate the conclusion. Interpret coefficients or fitted relationships and appropriate measures of uncertainty, model fit, or predictive performance. Discuss limitations and avoid unsupported causal claims.

A good project does not require a model with perfectly satisfied assumptions. If concerns remain, assess their consequences and report them clearly. Using a more complicated model is not automatically better.

For Option A, do not reuse a data set analyzed in class, the textbook, or homework assignments without Dr. Yu’s approval.

Possible sources of data include

  • TidyTuesday
  • Kaggle
  • Harvard Dataverse
  • UCI Machine Learning Repository
  • FiveThirtyEight.

You are welcome to find data elsewhere.

Option B — New Regression Method

Choose one regression method not covered in class and investigate what it offers beyond a familiar method such as ordinary least squares (OLS). Your project should:

  1. Explain the motivation. What limitation or modeling need does the new method address? In what settings might it be useful?
  2. Explain how the method works. Present its main idea, model or objective function, assumptions, and important parameters in language your classmates can understand. Use equations and diagrams when helpful; lengthy mathematical proofs are not required.
  3. Implement the method. Fit it using an appropriate R or Python package (or your own code). Explain key implementation choices, such as tuning parameters.
  4. Make an informative comparison. Compare the method with at least one regression approach learned in class, under conditions where the difference matters. Use a real-data analysis, a simulation study, or both; choose fair and relevant comparison criteria.
  5. Critically evaluate the results. Interpret the findings, identify strengths and weaknesses, and explain when you would—or would not—recommend the new method.

A literature review or software tutorial alone is not sufficient. The project must contain an original, reproducible computational demonstration. You do not need to invent a new statistical method or prove new theoretical results.

Possible topics include

  • ridge regression
  • lasso
  • robust regression
  • quantile regression
  • Poisson regression
  • regression trees
  • generalized additive models
  • Bayesian linear regression
  • Gaussian process regression
  • partial least squares regression.

You may propose another method; select a topic whose scope is manageable within the available time.

A useful Option B experiment might compare OLS and robust regression with and without outliers, or OLS and ridge regression under different degrees of multicollinearity. The aim is to explain why the methods behave differently, not merely announce which one wins.

Oral Presentations (All Group Projects)

Each group has 12-15 minutes, followed by 1–3 minutes for questions. Every member must speak and contribute meaningfully.

A clear presentation will usually follow this sequence:

  1. Motivation and goal: What are you trying to learn or solve?
  2. Data and/or method: What information or regression approach did you use, and why?
  3. Analysis and evidence: Show the most informative models, visualizations, diagnostics, or comparisons.
  4. Conclusions: What did you find? What are the main limitations?

Focus on explaining your decisions and results, not displaying large blocks of code or unedited software output. You should be able to answer questions about your modeling choices.

Questions and Participation

  • Every presenting group must prepare and ask at least one substantive question about another group’s project during the presentation session.

  • Dr. Yu will coordinate the Q&A order to make sure each group has an opportunity to ask its question.

  • MSSC 5780 students, including those completing individual projects, will participate in evaluating group presentations as described below.

  • Individual-project students are not required to give a presentation.

Written Report

Report Format

  • Length: 8–12 pages for the main report, including figures and tables but excluding the title page, references, and optional appendix. Aim for clarity rather than filling pages.
  • Text: 11- or 12-point font; 1.5 or double spacing; standard margins.
  • First page: Project title, author(s), course, project option, and date.
  • Figures/tables: Number and caption them; refer to them in the text.
  • References: Cite data sources, papers, books, software, code, and other materials you used.
  • Code: Submit as separate, runnable files. Do not paste lengthy code into the main report.
  • Submission: A PDF report plus reproducible code and the necessary data or data-access instructions.

Use the following outline as your report template. You may adjust section titles, but address all of the listed content.

Option A Report Outline — Data Analysis

1. Introduction
Introduce the problem, explain why it matters, and state your research question(s).

2. Data and Exploratory Analysis
Describe the data source, observations, variables, preparation, and key exploratory figures.

3. Regression Modeling and Diagnostics
Explain your initial model, important modeling decisions, relevant diagnostics, any model revisions, and comparisons of reasonable alternatives.

4. Results and Interpretation
Answer the research question using the fitted results. Report appropriate uncertainty or predictive evidence; explain what the findings mean in context.

5. Discussion and Conclusion
Summarize the conclusions, remaining assumptions or concerns, practical limitations, and potential improvements.

References
List all cited sources. An optional appendix may contain supporting material not essential to the main argument.

Option B Report Outline — New Regression Method

1. Introduction and Motivation
Introduce the new method, the problem it addresses, and why the problem matters.

2. Method and Intuition
Explain how the method works, its model or objective, assumptions, important parameters, and how it differs from a familiar regression method.

3. Computational Study
Describe your real-data analysis or simulation design, implementation, comparison method(s), and evaluation criteria. Explain why the comparison is fair and informative.

4. Results and Interpretation
Use informative plots and tables to compare the methods. Explain observed differences and whether the evidence supports your expectations.

5. Discussion and Conclusion
Summarize strengths, limitations, and the situations in which you would or would not recommend the method.

References
List all cited sources. An optional appendix may contain additional mathematical or computational details.

Code, Data, and Academic Integrity

All projects must be reproducible:

  • Submit the code used to produce the numerical results, tables, and figures in the presentation and/or report.
  • Include the data when permitted, or provide a stable source and clear instructions to obtain it.
  • Give brief instructions for running the analysis and identify important packages or dependencies. Fix random seeds for simulations or other randomized analyses when appropriate.
  • Clearly cite sources and acknowledge outside assistance. If you use generative AI, briefly disclose what you used it for and check the accuracy of all code, analyses, and explanations. You remain responsible for understanding and defending the work.

Evaluation and Grading

Dr. Yu determines final project grades, with evaluation input from MSSC 5780 students for group presentations. Graduate students do not grade their own group. Graduate evaluations provide structured feedback and inform the presentation assessment; the final decision is Dr. Yu’s. Grades are based on the criteria below, not mechanically assigned by group ranking.

The same general standards apply to Options A and B, but the evidence differs: Option A emphasizes sound data analysis and diagnostics; Option B emphasizes understanding and an informative comparison of regression methods.

Individual Contributions to Group Projects

  • Group members ordinarily receive the same group-performance score, but Dr. Yu may adjust individual grades when documented contributions differ substantially.

  • Each group member will identify the teammate who made the strongest contribution (do not nominate yourself), and may briefly describe how the work was shared. This feedback informs, but does not automatically determine, individual adjustments.

  • Every group member must take part in the work and, for group projects, present part of the results. A student who does not present without an approved exception may receive no credit for the presentation component.

Dr. Yu reserves the right to clarify or revise project procedures, with changes announced to the class.
